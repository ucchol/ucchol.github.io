Research

I study how limitations in an attacker’s decision-making affect cybersecurity, and how these behaviors can inform defensive strategies. My work combines behavioral experiments, cognitive models, and game-theoretic analysis.

Research interests

Cyber deception and security games

I develop models for deciding where to place decoys when a defender has only an approximate understanding of an attacker’s behavior. For my work on quantal Stackelberg deception, I built an equilibrium solver and a cyber–physical testbed based on real vulnerability records. I used them to study what happens when attackers choose among several plausible targets and when a defender’s assumptions about how attackers break ties overstate the protection a strategy actually provides. In related work, I helped develop and evaluate strategies that coordinate cyber and physical decoys. This lets us study how a defender can present a consistent picture across both layers and assess the benefit of coordinating deployments.

  • Quantal deception
  • Coordinated decoys

Attacker cognition and choice overload

I design experiments to understand how the choices attackers face affect the decisions they make. In our published study, we compared laptop purchasing with two cyber tasks, file exfiltration and port scanning. We varied both the number of options and the amount of information available about them, then examined how participants’ decision times and choices changed. The effects varied by task, which led me to ask what people actually attend to when a decision becomes complex. In follow-on work, I use discrete-choice and cognitive process models to study how people weigh competing attributes and when they simplify a decision by relying on a small number of cues.

  • Choice overload

Malware and malicious-domain detection

I study how detection models behave when they encounter threats outside their training experience. In one collaboration, I helped fine-tune and evaluate language models for detecting domains generated by malware and data exfiltration over DNS. Our domain evaluation covered 59 malware families and examined performance on families withheld from training. In separate work, I contributed to a cluster-based malware detection framework that learns boundaries around the representations of known families. Those boundaries help flag unfamiliar samples before assigning a family label. Across both lines of work, I focus on designing evaluations that reveal how a model responds when its training data offers limited guidance.

  • DNS detection
  • Novel malware

Quantal Stackelberg cyber deception

GameSec 2026 · Published study

How should a defender place decoys when its estimate of an attacker’s behavior is wrong? Our study compared quantal Stackelberg equilibrium (QSE) with strong Stackelberg equilibrium (SSE) on two cyber–physical networks built from real vulnerabilities. QSE improved realized defender utility in all 144 comparisons on the tested rationality grids. The study also found that SSE could lead against some attackers that concentrate on a hard best response.

Choice overload in cyber defense

GameSec 2025 · Published study

Can more options and more information slow decisions during a cyber attack? In this human-subject study, participants selected laptops, chose files to exfiltrate, and made choices in an Nmap port-scanning task. The published analysis found adverse effects of choice complexity, with patterns that varied by task.

Published paper · Code & analysis notebook

Network defense and containment planning

Open-source project · Synthetic containment study

When an alert suggests lateral movement, which response blocks the most risk while keeping critical services reachable? This exercise models five hosts across three zones, with a directed attack graph, noisy alerts, and a shared response budget. The demo ranks actions such as inspecting a host, restricting a link, or isolating a device and explains what each action protects.

NXDOMAIN Sentinel

Open-source research prototype · Alert-only pilot

NXDOMAIN Sentinel groups repeated failed DNS lookups into cases an analyst can investigate. It scores how generated a domain looks and independently measures how far it is from known malware families. The replay benchmark tests whether combining those signals with device behavior helps identify episodes the classifier misses.
